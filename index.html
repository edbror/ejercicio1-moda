Drift asks what is left of authorship once the author admits they are not in charge. Nothing here is recorded. Every grain of sound — each bell, each pad, even the space the sounds appear to inhabit — is synthesised the instant you hear it and will never recur in that exact form. There is no score, no loop, no end. It is not a recording of a process; it is the process, ongoing, indifferent to whether anyone is listening.

The sliders register a wish, not a command. The system blends your wish with its own slow wandering and a drift that pulls everything back toward an equilibrium you never set. The glowing marker on each control shows where the value actually went — the gap between intention and result, opening and closing, second by second. You are not the composer. You are weather pressure. You lean on the system, and it leans back.

Press Cosmos and the work hands its deepest decision — the key — to the Sun. It tunes itself to live solar-wind and geomagnetic data measured by spacecraft at the L1 Lagrange point, 1.5 million km sunward, where Earth's gravity and the Sun's cancel into stillness. The data arrives an hour old: you hear not the present but a message already in transit. When the magnetic field turns southward — the same event that lights the aurora — the harmony darkens. A geomagnetic substorm does not influence the music; it re-keys it, without consent. The composer is a star that has never heard of music.

Against an aesthetics of mastery, Drift proposes one of tending: not the artist as origin, but as one pressure among many in a field that exceeds them. It is most fully itself when left alone — drifting on a wind that was blowing long before there was anyone to name it.

You may nudge it. You will never hold it. That is the work.

# The Wind That Was Already Blowing

### A catalogue essay on *Drift* (2026)

---

#### I. A music with no past tense

Most sound we encounter is in the past tense. A recording is a decision someone
made once and sealed; to press play is to exhume it, identical, again and again.
We have grown so used to this that we mistake it for the nature of recorded
sound itself, when in fact it is only the nature of *recording* — a particular
technology of preservation that freezes a moment and lets us carry it around.

*Drift* refuses the past tense. It contains no recordings. Its bells are not
samples of bells; they are frequency-modulated sine tones brought into being at
the instant of audition and allowed to decay into nothing, never to return in
that precise shape. Its pads are stacks of detuned oscillators swelling beneath
a moving filter. Even the sense of *room* — the wash of reverberation that tells
your ear the sound is somewhere — is conjured from synthesised noise, a space
that has no physical existence. There is nothing here to exhume. The work is
present tense, continuously, and it does not loop. To listen is not to replay a
decision but to witness one being made, and unmade, in real time.

This is the first of the work's quiet refusals, and the ground for everything
that follows: a sound that is always happening, never having happened.

#### II. The honest slider

The interface is disarmingly conventional. Seven sliders, a couple of buttons.
The vocabulary of every synthesiser and mixing desk of the last seventy years
invites you, by sheer convention, to assume control. And then the work declines
to give it.

What the sliders register is not a command but a *wish*. The system takes your
wish and folds it into its own slow, autonomous wandering — a mean-reverting
random walk that drifts of its own accord and is forever pulled back toward an
equilibrium you never set. Push a value high and hold it there, and the
instrument will lean in your direction, but it will keep breathing around your
intention, sometimes drifting far from it, always returning on its own schedule
rather than yours.

The crucial decision is that the work makes this visible. A small luminous
marker on each control shows where the value has *actually* gone, as distinct
from where you placed the handle. The gap between the two opens and closes
continuously, all evening, a slow visual rendering of the difference between
intention and outcome. This is, I think, the most honest object in any gallery
of interactive art: a control that admits, in plain sight, that it is not in
control. You are not steering. You are applying pressure. The system applies
pressure back.

There is one moment of bluntness permitted — a *Gust*, which shoves the whole
system in a fresh random direction. It is the gesture of someone who, unable to
steer, settles for a shove. Even then, what you have changed is a tendency, not
a destination. The weather absorbs your shove and carries on being weather.

#### III. The composer is a star

The work's most radical move is to give away the decision that no instrument
usually surrenders: the key. The tonal centre — the ground on which all the
harmony stands — is, in *Drift*'s synced state, handed to the Sun.

Press *Cosmos*, and the instrument stops generating its own weather and begins
to listen, instead, to the real thing: live measurements of the solar wind, the
interplanetary magnetic field, and the planetary geomagnetic storm index. These
are read by spacecraft stationed at the **L1 Lagrange point** — a gravitational
null about 1.5 million kilometres sunward of Earth, where the pull of our planet
and the pull of the Sun cancel into a kind of stillness, and where, accordingly,
we have parked our instruments to watch the weather coming off the star before
it reaches us.

The mapping is not arbitrary; it is physical. When the Sun's magnetic field
turns *southward* — the precise condition under which solar plasma couples to
Earth's magnetosphere and ignites the aurora — the harmony of the piece
darkens, sliding toward the minor and the modal. A fast solar wind makes the
system restless and quickens its changes. A geomagnetic substorm, a sudden
violent reconfiguration of the field, does not gently influence the music: it
**re-keys** it, forcing an immediate change of tonal centre, without consent and
without warning. The same event that, on a clear night at high latitude, throws
green light across the sky, here throws the music into a new key.

And there is a tenderness in the latency. The data arrives roughly an hour after
the wind it describes left the Sun's vicinity; the star itself is eight light-
minutes away. So you are never hearing the present. You are hearing a message
already in transit when it reaches you, a sound that is structurally, physically
behind its cause. The composer of this music is ninety-three million miles away,
indifferent, and has never heard of music.

#### IV. An aesthetics of tending

It would be easy to read *Drift* as a work about powerlessness, but I think that
is the wrong word, or at least too small a one. The condition it stages is the
ordinary condition of contemporary life: we act inside systems — climatic,
economic, infrastructural, planetary — that are too large, too slow, and too
entangled for any of us to command, and yet our inputs are real. We are not
nothing. We are not sovereign either. We are pressures in a field.

What *Drift* proposes is not resignation but a different posture toward agency,
one we might call **tending**. The gardener does not command the garden; the
sailor does not command the wind. They read, they lean, they adjust, they accept
that the outcome is a negotiation with forces that exceed them and predate them.
This is, increasingly, the only honest model for action at scale — and it
asks of us an unfamiliar kind of attention, one that does not resolve into
mastery or payoff. To sit with *Drift* is to practise influencing something that
will carry on, unbothered and unrepeatable, long after you have closed it.

The work is most fully itself when no one is touching it at all — synced to the
sky, drifting on a wind that has been blowing since long before there was anyone
to name it, and will be blowing long after. You may nudge it. You will never
hold it.

That refusal is not a limitation of the work. It is the work.
